Geometric Algebra

Solving equations is the physical act of finding where paths meet.

The Rationale

In Algebraic Geometry, we move beyond seeing equations as "machines" that output values. Instead, an equation is a constraint. It describes the set of all physical points \((x, y)\) where a specific balance is maintained.

While \(y = mx + c\) is useful for functions, the form \(ax + by = c\) allows us to see the world as a grid of intersecting paths. To solve a system is simply to find the coordinate pair where two different constraints are satisfied simultaneously.

Choosing the Special Cases

The most efficient way to visualize a line is to find its anchors—the points where it intersects the axes of our coordinate system. We treat the axes as special constant constraints:

The y-axis is the line where \(x = 0\)
The x-axis is the line where \(y = 0\) (The Roots)

Case Study: \(5x + 2y = 10\)

By testing the two special cases, we find the "ends" of the line segment within the first quadrant:

1. Intersect with the y-axis (\(x=0\)):
\(5(0) + 2y = 10 \implies 2y = 10 \implies \mathbf{y = 5}\).
Anchor Point: (0, 5)
2. Intersect with the x-axis (\(y=0\)):
\(5x + 2(0) = 10 \implies 5x = 10 \implies \mathbf{x = 2}\).
Anchor Point: (2, 0)
